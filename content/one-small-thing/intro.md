title: One Small Thing

I’ve met a lot of teams and leaders who were sure that the way they worked was the only way to get things done.

I’ve been that person. I was sure story points didn’t work, until I met a team full of talented, smart, hardworking people who stuck with them and had massive success. It made me realize that the important question isn’t whether a process is universally right or wrong. 

Over a decade in the industry and I've seen countless ways teams work. Of course I've got my own tried and true flavor of SCRUM (it barely looks like SCRUM...) and I've been a part of and on the receiving end of others. Engineers, product managers, designers all show up to their new job and spend the first few weeks learning the way the team works. After a while, everyone working on that team just accepts the team's and company's operating system for what it is: fixed, immovable, and flawed but fine. Until its changed on you.

Most people experience workplace change as something that happens to them. A reorg, a new customer, a new priority: suddenly the way the team works is different, and you may not have had much say in it. You may not be able to control those bigger changes, but you can have some agency in how your team responds. And if you’re a manager, helping create that agency is part of your job.

Because circumstances do change. People on the team come and go. Customers change. Products and codebases grow. Teams move offices or go remote or return to the office. The way you work can make sense for a while and then stop making sense. If nobody feels able to say so, the process can keep going long after it’s helping. If the org doesn't have the muscle to contend with those changes, then they are addressed long after they start hurting.

A big part of a leader’s job is paying attention to how the team works, not just what it delivers. That means making it possible for people to flag problems early, talk about what’s working, and try a small adjustment without treating it like a grand transformation. The point isn’t to change things just to change them. It’s to take responsibility for how the team works and why.

So this is a series of 52 small things you or your team can try, one each week. Some are personal prompts to reflect on how you work. Some are small changes to things like how you run standup. Some will involve the team. They’re meant to be small enough to try without turning them into a major initiative.

I hope that after a few months, you’ll have more than a list of things you tried. I hope you’ll see that you can influence how your team works, that you can talk about what’s working and what isn’t, and that trying a small change doesn’t have to be a big, scary commitment.

And maybe you’ll start to notice more of what’s working, too. When a team pays attention to how it works, it has more chances to understand what helped, celebrate it, and carry it forward.

These small practices won’t make every workplace problem easy to solve. But they can help a team get better at noticing what needs attention, taking ownership where it can, and adapting when things change. That’s part of creating the conditions for healthy teams.

I might me asking a lot of you for the first week. I want you to look at your calendar and think about each meeting. Open a notebook or a blank doc and answer three questions about each of the meetings you and your team have this week:

1. Who defined how this meeting gets run?
2. Has the way the meeting gets run ever changed?
3. Is the meeting serving its purpose?

Don't spend anytime thinking about what you would change, or how to fix it, or even if the meeting should be canceled. Just reflect on these questions and write your thoughts down some where.
