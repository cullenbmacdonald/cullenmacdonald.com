One of the biggest-impact things you can do as a leader is make sure your team has a way to reflect on itself. And that they can do it in a (sorry, it’s cliché, but) psychologically safe way is critical to getting anything useful out of it. People need to feel like they can say something isn’t working, even when the thing that isn’t working was your idea.

After enough years, the processes, routines, and decisions about how the team works really pile up. It’s easy to pick apart decisions someone else made, but it can feel pretty bad to hear that something you suggested, maybe something you put a lot of work into, is making someone else’s job harder. If you want people to keep telling you those things, how you respond matters.

This week, we’re going to start in your one on ones. If you’re not doing regular one on ones well then that’s the first change we’re going to make. You don’t need to do them weekly, but they should be regularly scheduled and you should protect them on your cal, especially when things get busy and you get double booked.

Often, managers use one on ones to get status on projects and find out where things are blocked, by whom, and what needs to happen next. There are plenty of other meetings where those conversations happen, (ahem) Standup or sprint plannings or a project check-in or whatever. Instead, this week I want you to use your one onone to give your direct report room to say how they’re feeling. You should do this in peer one onones, too.

Ask how they’re doing. What’s on their mind? Where are they feeling stuck? And give them time to answer, because if you’ve spent the last six months asking for project updates, it might take a little while for them to believe you’re asking about something else.

Talk about the good. Talk about the things they’re excited about. Give them room to talk about things they don’t really understand or aren’t sure how to do yet. You can offer your own versions of those stories, too, because it helps to hear that the person managing you also gets stuck and doesn’t have everything figured out.

The important thing this week is to pay attention to what you do when they tell you something uncomfortable. Maybe they’re frustrated with how work gets assigned, or they feel like they can’t get a decision made, or a meeting you thought was helping is taking up a lot of their time. It’s really easy to jump in with the context they’re missing or explain why you made that decision, and maybe there is context they’re missing, but resist the urge to explain it away. Sit there and hear it and say you're hearing it and you'll get back to them with your thoughts after the one on one (and mean it!). The follow through on that promise to get back to them is crucial to building the feedback loop of trust. 

It’s also really easy to start looking for things to fix, especially when you care about someone and you have some ability to help. But if every feeling they share immediately becomes an action item, you can end up deciding what to do before you’ve understood what they were trying to tell you.

So sit in the conversation for a little while. Ask another question. Make sure you understand what they mean, including how it’s affecting them, before deciding what you’re going to do about it. There might be something you need to act on, and you should follow through when there is, but you don’t need every one on one to end with a list of things to change.

Next week we’ll start talking about Retros, where you’re going to ask the whole team to reflect together. This week though, give yourself some practice hearing what one person has to say without immediately needing to explain, defend, or fix something. Especially if the thing they’re talking about is something you think rocks or came up with yourself.
