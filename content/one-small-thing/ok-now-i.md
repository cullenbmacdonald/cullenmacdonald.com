This week I’m going to ask you to do the hardest possible thing imaginable: changing your mind.

So far, with regards to Standup, we’ve started to notice what our teammates say, encouraged everyone, including ourselves. to pick one thing that matters most and have that be our only goal, and then spent a week making sure to call out what we need from each other to get that one goal done.

What’s the point of this daily ritual so many teams perform? Is it simply to say out loud what we’re doing? To prove how busy we are? To establish accountability for our tasks at hand? Certainly not. Or certainly that isn’t the goal we’re getting at here. Last week, when we banned the phrase “no blockers,” we got close to what I believe this whole exercise is aimed at.

Teams exist for a reason. Big, complicated projects are easier to tackle together, and they can be more fun that way, too. Something we'll come back to is the belief that the fastest way to finish the work isn’t for everyone to keep making progress on their own thing, but for someone to jump in and help a teammate get theirs over the line.

As you enter Standups this week, go first, and then listen to the rest of the team. Use what you’re learning **during the standup** to figure out if someone else is blocked by something **you can unlock**. After they say what they need and before the next person goes, change your mind. Something like, “Ok, actually the most important thing for me to do today is review Julia’s PR.”

This is a different way of thinking for some teams. If you’re the manager, it can have a huge impact on your directs. You’ve given them permission to reduce their daily goals and commitments to one. Now you’re giving them permission for that one goal not to be forward progress on some Jira ticket they’ve been assigned, but to plug into and unblock a teammate’s work.

This sets us down a long path we’ll explore over the next few weeks, but the important thing this week is to think about the biggest impact you can make to the team, not just your own work, and how you’re modeling that behavior for the folks who report to you.
